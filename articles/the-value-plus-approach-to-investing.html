The Value-Plus Approach To Investing

An Extension Of The Traditional Value-Investing Approach

From June 2018 to November 2019, I employed a strategy characterized by holding a handful of cheap stocks, by standards of market valuation. Over that 17-month period, I returned -4.5% annualized, compared to the 11.8% total return achieved by the S&P 500. Although I typically advocate for a minimum 36-month period to evaluate performance, and I was not even halfway there yet, it became clear that this initial strategy was inadequate.

For the next five years, from November 2019 to September 2024, I shifted to a new strategy characterized by holding far fewer stocks—often a single stock—purchased on the basis of its relative market-price discount to "what I estimate would be a good price to pay for it". This second strategy is what I now call the "value-plus" approach to investing.

Value investing has gone through two dominant phases of understanding, which I term the "traditional" and the "modern" interpretations. Here, we review how the idea of value investing has evolved over time and how I have extended it to suit today's markets.

Traditional Value Investing

Value investing was popularized by Benjamin Graham who was a fund manager and a professor at Columbia Business School in New York City. Notably, Ben was one of Warren Buffett's early mentors and teachers. In his 1934 book titled Security Analysis, Ben defined a concept which would be known as "value investing", but which he referred to only as "investing":

An investment operation is one which, upon thorough analysis, promises safety of principal and a satisfactory return. Operations not meeting these requirements are speculative.

Supposing that nobody would want to achieve insecurity of capital and unsatisfactory return, upon poor or incomplete analysis, what Ben described as "investing" referred to any strategy that repeatably earns positive returns—indeed, by this traditional definition, and as later summarized by Warren's long-time partner Charlie Munger: all good investing is value investing.

Security Analysis, and its 1949 sequel The Intelligent Investor, were unique for their time, and still now, in placing focus on market price; not as an input for analysis, but as a tool for profit. Ben called for thorough analysis of a company's financial accounts with the goal of determining 1) the soundness of the business in terms of its ability to distribute earnings far into the future and 2) discerning, roughly, what that stream of distributions could be worth, to estimate the fair value of the entire business. However, in an effort to profit from this insight gained through analysis, Ben uniquely called for the intelligent investor to only purchase securities at market prices far below that estimated fair value of the business—that is, although the market price of the security should not enter into the investor's analysis, it becomes a critical tool for profiting on that analysis. Although modern investment firms have widely adopted the financial accounting analysis Ben taught, most continue to ignore the latter part of his teaching regarding prices, which has been more important for ensuring both safety of principal and a satisfactory return throughout the history of markets.

"Value" In Modern Terms

The later 20th century brought quantitative methods for synthesizing large amounts of data. Tremendous effort was poured into uncovering the statistical essence of the value approach and it was found that Ben's methods could be imitated by purchasing stocks which traded at low price-to-earnings (P/E) and price-to-book-value (P/B) multiples. Of course, this insertion of market price into the ex-ante analysis was a gross contortion of the traditional idea of "value".

Today, the word "value" evokes investors who scrape the proverbial trash bin for cheap stocks, oftentimes regardless of the business's earning power or financial accounts, holding on to them for dear life, trapped into losing investments for long periods without leveraging short-term market-price fluctuations for profit. This is where the term value trap comes from.

Further analysis by non-practitioners "revealed" that value was one of many "factors" affecting returns—along with company size, company quality, momentum, and volatility. This led to "value" becoming instrumental to a broader investment approach, rather than constituting a complete strategy in and of itself. As a result, it no longer matters if the modern value investor makes money or not—it only matters if a manager can build a sound quantitative emulation of the value factor, such that a portfolio-level determination can set the desired "allocation to value". And so, many managers have sullied the "value" brand by underperforming benchmarks with a strategy that has lost its original meaning. If all good investing is value investing and you're underperforming, then whatever you're doing cannot be value investing.

The Value-Plus Approach To Investing

I created the value-plus approach with the goal of returning to the demonstrably successful traditional value-investing approach introduced by Ben Graham, while addressing some practical questions that he left unanswered for the portfolio manager.

Value-plus shares the traditional goal of achieving safety of principal and satisfactory returns upon thorough analysis. In most cases, that translates roughly into plain language as: try not to lose the money, try to make enough additional money, and try to do it consistently.

The Diversification Problem

I believe the world is abundant: the baker, the miner, and the banker all have to make ends meet, and you can make a lot of money culturing yeast or selling rocks. But, it's hard to make a lot of money culturing yeast and selling rocks, because resources are limited. Similarly, if I'm fairly certain of the relative attractiveness of each stock, then it would be foolish to spend my limited capital buying any except the best one. If we accept this predicate, then risk comes from uncertainty in one's judgement of securities, given data available today, rather than from uncertainty in future economic variables. Risk-management then becomes an art requiring discipline in analyzing existing data, rather than one of predicting those economic variables, or even a matter of skill, since there are so many stocks that one can simply ignore any too difficult to evaluate, confident in the eventual discovery of stocks falling within one's circle of competence. For this reason, except for funds of considerable size or in unique arbitrage situations, the value-plus approach to investing requires holding no more than one position.

The Holding Period Problem

Some people are convinced that cash is capable of earning returns, but I have a $5 bill sitting on my desk and I can tell you it was a $5 bill three years ago and it will still be a $5 bill three years from now. Cash earns 0% returns and has no value-in-use. Bank-sponsored chequing and savings accounts promising yield are not, strictly speaking, cash or cash equivalents—they are banking products. Although rare, the sponsoring banks can collapse, as can the deposit insurance programs purporting to cover them. Even treasuries have price volatility and duration risk. The only value cash—real cash—provides in investing is the option to exchange it for any other security, an option which is only valuable when it can be exercised—or, in other words, when another security is imminently purchasable. All that to say: the value-plus approach calls for the sale of securities only to fund the purchase of other, more attractive securities.

The corollary is that if a sale marks the end of a holding period and a sale is only reasonable when other securities are more attractive to own, then the holding period depends on the rate at which other securities become more attractive to own. Unfortunately, I've yet to figure out a way to foresee such price movements. Call me if you find a way and we'll both make a lot of money.

In the value(-plus) approach, holding periods and time horizons are non-sensical. Just as physicists discovered that, in quantum environments, you must choose exclusively between certainty of position or momentum, but not both; in market environments, you must choose exclusively between certainty of time or price, but not both. Value-plus places extreme emphasis on certainty of purchase price and no emphasis on certainty of timing. And, it goes without saying that, in public markets, you don't ever get to dictate your sale price. People tend to think about investing in terms of time horizons: weeks, months, quarters, years, decades; but I argue that is arbitrary, limiting, and ineffective—I believe that thinking in terms of prices is far better. Therefore, the value-plus approach eschews any concept of a holding period.

The Faith Problem

Someone once said to me: "a smart man knows things and a wise man knows that he does not know things." Ben leans on his formidable experience to persuade his reader of the soundness of his investment practices, I'm not so easily convinced. I had to try it for myself, which is why I started investing in the first place. Still, even with the track record I've accrued, I don't know if it will work. Tomorrow might be the day markets close for good.

What I do know is that investing is about 1) having capital, 2) using that capital to buy things, and 3) doing that over and over to have more capital than you started with. Sure, I can put effort into soliciting new investors, as many managers choose to do, but if returns are poor, nobody will want to invest in my fund. At least in theory, if returns are stupendous, then everybody should want to invest in my fund. I believe the best use of my effort is to get really good at using capital to buy things. Currently, the value-plus approach is the best way I know to buy things. But that might change, as it did in November 2019. I don't know what the future brings. All I can do is try to get better every day and hope that "buying things" remains a profitable endeavour.
